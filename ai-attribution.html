Observability is not Attribution

Complex agents introduce variance, challenging the role of observability alone

A well-structured substrate and compulsive versioning allow statistical analysis of every agentic and LLM-as-a-judge call, generating honest adjudication and evidence-backed triage.

What moved with a changed score?

A value changing is not yet an answer. Holonograph decomposes a score by attributing each part to your agent, the model, or your eval, and separates real change from noise, quantifying how much each source changed with that number. This decomposition is the method under patent application. Substrate labeling further identifies which component of your agent’s substrate contributed to the agent’s part: lessons, skills, code, and prompt are each separated and attributed.

The longitudinal substrate

Attribution requires memory

A score can change while the behavior stays the same. When the model is updated, or the lens that scores it is revised, the metric drifts while the underlying behavior does not change. Holonograph versions every lens and keeps the full history, so each reading is checked against the exact lens that produced it. The outcome is a clear behavioral attribution to your agent, the model, or your eval.

The model you asked for and the model that answered are two different facts. A vendor can re-route behind an unchanged alias. Holonograph records both sides, so that change has a name instead of showing up as unexplained drift. The judge has the same two sides.

The attribution matrix

A release changed several scores. The matrix reads each declared dimension against the axis of each source, in the terms the dashboard uses: the gap in points, whether chance explains it, and what stands in the way of attribution. A gap is a candidate. Attribution holds only where the record separates the sources.

Rows

Dimensions as declared in the lens, such as title_quality or tool_discipline.

Columns

Each source is read on its own axis: the substrate, light_source_served_model, judge identity (rubric · model · scope), and the noise floor.

Cells

The gap in points between groups on that axis. A gap is a candidate for attribution, not a causal share. Brighter cells hold larger gaps.

Not chance

A gap this wide would show up by chance less than once in 1,000 samples. Here, tool_discipline changed with the substrate.

Orthogonality

The axis was declared one that shouldn’t affect the score, and the score changed with it.

Confounded axis

The light source and the judge changed in the same window, so the title_quality gap cannot be split between them. Separating the two takes its own probe.

Concentrated, smooth

From the judge transfer curve. Concentrated: most scores land on a single value, and the judge is barely discriminating. Smooth: scores spread across many values, a graded response.

Within noise

A gap smaller than the noise floor for that dimension.

Noise floor

Per-call variation, measured as a calibrated floor for each dimension.

Facets of rubric, judge, and context scope

Lens facets

When a rubric stops discriminating

When one judge scores many concerns at once, scores can concentrate at an anchor. A ten and a nine stop being different readings, and a failure shows only that something went wrong. Holonograph reports that condition on the event. Where a rubric resolves into facets, each is attributed on its own, and where two change together the reading is marked confounded.

The multiplexer

One lens reads every vendor

One call can land on several vendors at once: a primary whose reply the agent uses, and observers running the same input in parallel. The agent never knows. A change of vendor becomes a comparison on the same terms, and your baseline carries over.

Each vendor’s cost and latency arrive on the event with no operator work, and every result is graded on the same terms. Price sits next to judged quality for the same inputs, so a cheaper model can be evaluated on what it costs in judgment before any traffic moves.
